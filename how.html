What it is

Civic Accord is a free, nonpartisan tool for a two-way relationship between residents and their governments. It shows what your governments have open for public input. It lets verified voters talk an issue through, finds where people with different views agree, shows what each choice would cost, and sends the result to the office that represents them. The office is asked to answer in public.

It works for any U.S. address and every level of government: federal, state, county, city or town, and school district. It is advisory. It doesn't replace voting or elections, and it doesn't make laws.

Proposals come only from voters (planned). Once real deliberations run, a proposed bill or policy will go to an office only as the result of verified voters in that place talking it through together. Civic Accord itself never takes a side on any bill. When you use it to write to an official about a bill, the view is yours.

One exception, said openly. Civic Accord does ask for one thing: that offices offer residents a public, checkable way to work with them, like this tool or any tool meeting the same standards. The sample "Ask your representatives" page and the sample national deliberation are about that. Until a neutral host runs Civic Accord and its lawyers review it, the Ask page isn't linked from the menus. Because Civic Accord benefits if offices say yes, this is a conflict of interest, and we say so here. The sample's result is an office policy, a practice an office can adopt on its own, not a bill.

The six steps

  1. Learn. Everyone reads the same short facts. Every fact links to its source.
  2. Weigh in. Verified voters agree, disagree, or pass on short statements, and can add their own. There's no reply button, so no one can pile on. Votes count only after you press Submit, and you get a receipt (sample, in this example).
  3. Common ground. The tool shows what people with different views agree on, and where they split.
  4. Weigh costs. Each option shows its total cost, its cost per resident, and how the estimate was made. You can turn options on and off and see the total change.
  5. Draft. A starting bill is drafted from the common ground. In this example, simple code assembles it; in the live version, AI would help write it. Ideas that divide people are flagged, not hidden.
  6. Hand off. The package goes to the office. You can follow what happens next on a public timeline.

Who can take part

Verified voters only. Only registered voters can vote or add statements in a deliberation, so every voice is a real, verified voter. Anyone can read everything, see what's open for public input, comment through official channels, and write to their representatives.

Two checks. First, that an active voter record exists for you in the district. Second, that you're really that voter: a code is mailed to the home address on your voter record (not one you type), or staff at a library or election office compare your ID with the record. An instant yes-or-no check by your state election office, and state digital IDs where law allows, are planned. Each state's rules on voter records decide what may be used. In this example, no real check happens.

Planned: faster ways to prove you're you. A mailed code takes a week or more. Beside it and the in-person check, the plan is to offer faster options, in this order of preference: a state digital ID (a mobile driver's license or state ID app) that can confirm your name and address without showing anything else; a commercial identity service, such as ID.me, only if the host approves it after review, and only as a choice beside the free options, never the only way; and Login.gov, which federal law limits to government agencies, so it can be used only through a state election office that adopts it for its own voter check. In every case the identity service tells Civic Accord only that the name and address matched. The match to an active voter record is still done by the host or the election office, your identity stays separate from your votes, and no address is stored. Votes made before a check is finished are labeled pending and counted only once it succeeds.

North Dakota has no voter registration, so there's no voter record to check there. The check would follow North Dakota's own rules for proving who can vote; that is still to be worked out in the state's legal review.

Only your own governments. You can respond only where you're a voter: federal items from anywhere, your state's items only if you're a voter in that state, and local items only in your own county, city, town, or township. You can still read everything from everywhere.

Today, this uses the state or address you chose on the home page or My representatives, which stays on your device. A state alone is enough to respond to that state's bills; an address adds your local governments. Nothing checks it yet. Planned: once real voter checks exist, it will use your checked districts.

Not a voter yet? Civic Accord Learn is a separate space for young people and anyone who can't vote. It uses real issues and points to real actions anyone can take. You can tap the topics you care about or search, using the same open word lists as Issues, and your picks stay on your device. Nothing there is counted in deliberations.

Your privacy

  • The address you type never reaches Civic Accord's servers. Your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau's free lookup to find your districts. Only your districts are kept. (In the live version, the voter list used to mail codes is handled only by the host or the election office, and each address is deleted after printing.)
  • Your identity is kept apart from your votes. In the live version, the voter check gives your device a sealed voting key. The vote record sees only that key, never your name. It's designed so even the host can't link the two.
  • Messages to your representatives come from you. Email opens in your own email app. Civic Accord never sees or keeps what you send.
  • Looking up your legislators sends only your state, never an address or district. Your browser picks out your own legislators.
  • Today, what you do stays in this browser: your districts, the issues you follow, your records of what you sent, reactions, practice votes, your steps on each item (read, said where you stand, spoke up), your name and town for messages, topics, notes, builder drafts, the sample office inbox, Learn's picks, sample verification, and whether you've seen the welcome card. Civic Accord never receives your address, your messages, or your views. To show live information, your browser asks Civic Accord's data service for public data, such as the list for your state or the latest record of an issue you follow. Like any website, the hosting service briefly logs those requests, including your internet address. On a shared computer, use Forget me on this device at the bottom of any page to remove everything Civic Accord saved in this browser, including the offline copy of pages and the name and town you used in messages.
  • No ads or analytics. The site doesn't track you. Its fonts are served from civicaccord.org itself, so no font service sees your visit.
  • Outside services your browser contacts. The U.S. Census Bureau (only when you look up an address; it receives that address). GitHub, only if you choose "Suggest a better topic" under "Wrong topic?", which opens a GitHub issue form with the item's title. Civic Accord's own data service, which gets official information from Congress.gov, the Federal Register, Open States, and local agenda systems. Your address is never sent there. If that service is down, your browser may get the list of members of Congress straight from the public congress-legislators project on GitHub.

How votes are counted

In the live version, votes go into a public record that can be added to but never quietly changed. Simple code that anyone can read does the counting, so anyone can download the record and recount it.

  • Your receipt code (also called a tracking code) shows that your sealed vote is in the record. It's designed so it can't show how you voted, so it can't be used to buy your vote or prove it to someone pressuring you. Before you submit, you'll be able to spot-check that the tool sealed your choices correctly.
  • You can change your votes until the deliberation closes. Only your last votes count. This is designed to make pressure much harder, because someone else can't easily tell whether you changed them later.
  • Votes stay sealed until the end. When a deliberation closes, the sealed votes are shuffled in a way anyone can check, so they can't be matched to receipts. Then they're opened, counted, and published for anyone to recount.

Two questions are still open for the independent security review before launch:

  • Whether groups and common ground can be shown while a deliberation is open, or only when it closes. Showing them early could reveal how some people voted. This sample shows them right away.
  • How much vote-by-vote detail to publish. With many statements, an unusual pattern of votes could stand out. One option being studied is showing each voter only some of the statements.

The plan is to use established, open methods rather than inventing new ones: sealing and receipt codes like Microsoft's ElectionGuard (its code is open for anyone to read), plus a separate, checkable shuffle.

Each voter record can be claimed by only one verified person. Before a package goes to an office, voter status is checked again against the current list.

Protecting your voter record (planned). In about half the states, anyone can get a copy of the voter list, and others limit it to campaigns, parties, or officials (each state's rules). People also move. So the mailed code is protected in several ways:

  • Codes go only to records the election office lists as active, at the address on that record. Civic Accord adds no addresses and changes no voter records.
  • The code comes in a sealed letter. The outside doesn't mention voting. The letter tells the voter what to do if they didn't ask for it. A code works once, expires in 30 days, and locks after 5 wrong tries.
  • If someone else claimed your record, you can take it back: in person at a library or election office, through the election office, or with a take-back code mailed to your address. Help will be offered for people who can't travel, including at care homes. Their claim is cancelled, and the system is designed so their votes can be removed if the deliberation hasn't closed. How to do that without linking anyone to their votes is still being reviewed.
  • Some voters can't get mail at the address on their record: people in address confidentiality programs, military and overseas voters, and people without home mail delivery. They'll use the election office or an in-person check instead.
  • Limits stop mass requests: one code per voter record a month, and no more codes to one address than it has active voters. Every request gets the same answer, so the limits don't reveal who lives where.
  • Public counts for each district, published weekly once they're large enough not to point to anyone: codes mailed, records claimed, claims taken back, and claims cancelled. Anyone can watch for unusual jumps.
  • Spot checks for fake sign-ups: verified voters can never outnumber registered voters, but that alone wouldn't catch much. So each district also shows what share of its registered voters have verified. An independent auditor writes to a random sample of verified voter records to confirm the voter really signed up, and publishes how many did. Claims on records the election office later removes are counted and published too.

Today: the example uses sample votes, and receipts are generated on your device to show how they would work.

How common ground is found

Participants are sorted into groups by how similarly they vote. The live version will use the open method from Pol.is, which Taiwan's vTaiwan process uses. In the example, the groups come with the sample data. Each statement is then labeled with these exact rules:

LabelRule
Common groundAt least 70% of every group with at least 10 votes agrees.
Divides groupsAt least 70% of one group agrees, and under 50% of another does.
Still formingAnything else, or fewer than two groups with at least 10 votes each on that statement.

A group only counts toward a label once at least 10 of its members have voted on that statement. That way, a few people can't speak for a whole group. A "pass" counts as not agreeing.

Guarding against small, organized groups. Common ground means agreement among the people who took part, not the whole district. So:

  • Results show how many verified voters took part, and what share of the registered voters that government represents. Offices see the same numbers.
  • The randomly chosen citizen panel is shown beside every result. If the open results and the panel disagree, the tool says so.
  • Planned: a public timeline of how many votes arrived each day, so a sudden rush is easy to spot.

Citizen panels. A randomly chosen panel, balanced to match the district by age and area, votes too. Its result is shown beside the open results, using these rules: the panel agrees at 70% or more, is divided at 40 to 69%, and disagrees under 40%. When the panel and the open group differ, the tool says so. A table shows who took part compared with the district.

Rules for statements, and who reviews deliberations

These rules are planned for the live version. They're about behavior, never about which side someone is on. Any view on the issue is welcome, including strong criticism of officials or of Civic Accord. When in doubt, a statement stays up. The final wording will be checked in the host's legal review, so it follows free-speech law.

What a statement can't include. A moderator holds back a statement that:

  • shares private details about a person, like a home address or phone number
  • threatens anyone or calls for violence
  • uses a slur against a person or group, or harasses a person
  • advertises, asks for money, or campaigns for or against a candidate or party. "Senator X's bill would cost too much" is fine. "Vote Senator X out" is not.
  • pretends to be an official or an office
  • doesn't address this deliberation's question, or repeats a statement that's already there (repeats are combined, with a note)

How moderation works.

  • New statements are checked by a trained moderator before others see them, usually within a day. Moderators aren't told who wrote a statement. New statements stop two days before a deliberation closes, so every statement gets checked in time.
  • Every statement held back is listed publicly with the rule it broke. For ads, campaigning, repeats, and off-question statements, the text is shown too. For anything else, only the rule is shown, so harmful words aren't spread.
  • The author gets a private code with each statement and can use it to appeal. A different moderator decides.
  • Each voter can add a limited number of statements per deliberation, so no one can flood it. How to count this without linking statements to voters is still being worked out.
  • AI may point out possible repeats or rule problems to a moderator. It never removes, edits, or approves a statement. A person decides.
  • An independent reviewer checks a sample of moderation decisions and publishes what they find.

Who reviews a deliberation before it opens. Anyone can set one up with the builder, so each one is checked first:

  • Two reviewers are drawn at random, one from each of two partner groups with different points of view. The list of partner groups is public, so anyone can see the balance. Reviewers disclose any personal or financial stake in the issue, and anyone with a stake steps aside.
  • They check that every fact has a source, current law is stated correctly, costs say how they were made, and the starting statements cover the main views fairly, including the strongest case for each side.
  • The review is published: the reviewers' roles, what they checked, what they asked to change, and any stakes disclosed. The pack's author discloses their own stake too.
  • Before it opens, anyone can raise a public objection for 7 days. Each objection gets a public answer.
  • The convener who started Civic Accord has no role in reviews, and discloses any stake like anyone else.

Today: the sample shows new statements right away, on your device only. None of this review exists yet. It needs a host.

How costs are shown

Every option shows how much, times the cost of each unit, equals the total. For example: 12 inspectors × $85,000 = $1.02 million a year. It also shows the cost per resident, using the official population figure. Every estimate says how it was made and links to its source. Yearly and one-time costs are kept separate. Fees and fines that could pay for it are subtracted only when there's a reliable estimate. Anything that can't be estimated is shown as "not yet estimated," never guessed.

Every AI summary links to the votes behind it, so you don't have to take it on trust.

The office, not the person

The conversation is with an office (a seat), not an individual politician. In the live version, responses will be signed by the office and give the role of the person who approved them (for example, chief of staff), not their name. When someone new is elected, every open package, request, and past response passes to them. Offices answer publicly with a reason. They can ask for changes, which residents then weigh in on, and they report each step: introduced, heard, voted. Offices can also ask their district to deliberate, through a neutral host. There's no party branding, no campaign use, and no ratings of officials.

How an office proves it's really the office (planned). A fake reply from an office would do real harm, so posting for an office is locked down:

  • The host confirms each office through official channels. It writes to the office at the contact listed in the government's own directory and asks the office to name an administrator.
  • Staff sign in with an official email address (for example, one ending in .gov) plus a passkey or security key: a sign-in tied to their own phone or a small device, so a stolen password isn't enough. Offices without official email, like many small towns and special districts, are confirmed by a letter on official letterhead or at a public meeting, and their responses say so. Campaign email addresses and accounts are never accepted.
  • Every response shows how the office was confirmed and the role of the person who approved it, such as chief of staff. A public list shows every change to who can post for an office.
  • Each response has a permanent page on civicaccord.org. If you see an office "response" anywhere else, check it there.
  • If an office says it didn't send something, the response is marked "Under review" right away, and the host looks into it in public. The result is published either way: "Confirmed sent by the office" or "Not sent by the office." An office can't make a real response disappear by disowning it.
  • When a new officeholder takes the seat, the old staff's access ends that day, and the new office is confirmed the same way. Everything sent to the seat stays with it. Until the new office is confirmed, responses can't be posted and "No response yet" keeps counting. The same applies to vacancies and appointed replacements.
  • Civic Accord will never ask an office for a password by email or phone.

Issues: where items come from

Issues (once called Open Now) lists what your governments have open for public input. It comes straight from these sources, with no AI involved, and refreshes every few hours:

WhatSource
Proposed federal rules open for public commentFederal Register
Recently active bills in CongressCongress.gov. The 100 most recently updated bills. Bills that became law, were vetoed, or failed are left out, and so are resolutions that have been fully agreed to.
Bills still moving in your state legislatureOpen States. Shown only while the legislature is in session. Lists are downloaded on a schedule (about every 3 hours while a legislature is in session, about once a day otherwise) and saved, and every download shares one daily limit so Civic Accord stays within Open States' free use. Each list holds up to the 60 most recently active bills, or fewer (as few as 20) when many legislatures are in session at once; the page says how many there are in all. A saved list more than 36 hours old isn't shown, because bills on it may have been signed or vetoed since. Bills that were signed, vetoed, failed, or withdrawn are left out. A bill isn't left out just because an amendment or motion on it failed.
Local council meetings in the next 30 daysEach government's own public agenda system. Connected so far, as pilots: the Maui County Council (Hawaiʻi) and the Oakland City Council (California), including their committees and other public meetings. They were picked because their agenda systems offer public data with no key needed, and to test one county and one city. Most governments using the same agenda system can be added with one entry in the open registry. Shown when you pick that government's state.
Other city, county, and school board agendasComing as more governments are added

Each source shows when its list was fetched, and says so if a refresh just failed and an older list is being shown. Deadlines are checked against today's date every time the list is drawn, so an item never shows as open after its deadline, even from a saved copy. Official deadlines usually give only a date, so on the last day an item says "Closes today" and points to the notice for the exact time. Dates follow your device's time zone; federal comment deadlines are usually the end of the day Eastern time, so check the notice if you're farther west.

Each item links to the official text and the government's own comment channel. Some proposed rules have no online comment form. For those, the button says "How to comment (in the notice)" and opens the notice, which explains how to comment, often by email or mail.

Finding what you care about

There can be hundreds of items open at once. On your first visit, Issues asks you to pick up to five topics, like housing, schools, or farms and food. Then it shows what's open on those topics, with the soonest deadlines at the top, ten at a time. Meetings of your own county, city, or town are always included, since meeting titles rarely name a topic. Once you've chosen your state, federal items whose official title names one other state (an air plan for North Carolina, say) come after your own state's items and nationwide rules, and are labeled with that state; nothing is hidden. This reads only the title, with a plain list of state names, not AI. You can switch topics, see everything, or search everything at any time. When an agency posts many near-identical rules at once (for example, dozens of aircraft safety notices), they're folded into one line you can open.

Your next step on each item

Every item shows a short path: read it, say where you stand, speak up, and go further. The next step is always clearly marked. "Read what it says" opens the item's own page, built from the official summary, with the full official text one link away. For a proposed rule, speaking up means the official comment to the agency, because the agency must consider the official comments. For a bill, it means writing to the people who represent you. Going further means talking it through: weighing in on each part, copied word for word from the official summary (how that works). Today your votes there are practice, kept on your device. You can also build your own deliberation, which neighbors can join once a host reviews and opens it. The last step, having verified voters' views counted in a public record, is planned and marked that way. A step is marked done only when you say so, for example "Yes, I sent it," which also offers to keep a record of what you sent (how records work). Your steps are kept only on your device, as a reminder for you. They aren't counted or sent anywhere.

Items are sorted into topics by a published list of words, not by AI. For example, an item that mentions "tenant" or "landlord" goes under Housing. The title and the office are checked first. The longer summary is checked only if those match nothing. Each item shows which word put it there, so you can see why it's showing. Anyone can read the word lists (lib/topics.js) and suggest a fix. A word list can miss things or file something in the wrong place, so "Everything" and search always show every item. Your topic picks stay on your device and are never sent anywhere.

Weigh in on any bill or proposed rule, part by part

Every item listed on Issues has its own page where you can weigh in on it: bills in Congress, bills in your state legislature during its session, proposed federal rules, and connected local council meetings. For a local meeting, the parts are the agenda items with legislation attached, by their official titles. These pages are built automatically, only from official material, so no one has to write them and nothing is reworded:

  • The parts you weigh in on are copied word for word from the official summary, in order. For bills in Congress, that's the summary by the nonpartisan Congressional Research Service. For state bills, it's the summary the legislature posted, collected by Open States. Headings and exact repeats are left out, and the page says how many headings. When the official summary is only one or two short parts (many proposed rules), the page skips part-by-part practice voting and points to the official comment instead; a page with no official summary offers the whole text as one choice. Lines that introduce a list stay with each item, and background notes (like "As background" or "Existing law") are shown with the part they explain. If there's no official summary yet, you weigh in on the bill as a whole.
  • Costs come only from official estimates: the Congressional Budget Office for Congress, or the state's own fiscal note. If there isn't one, the page says "not yet estimated."
  • Facts like status, sponsors, and the official text come from the same sources as Issues and are refreshed about twice a day. The page shows when they were fetched. Party is never shown.
  • You can add your own statements. In the live version, they're checked against the published rules before others see them.

When the official text changes. Each practice vote is saved with the exact words it was cast on. If a summary is updated, your votes on unchanged parts stay, and votes on parts that changed or were removed are never moved onto the new text. The page tells you what changed so you can vote again.

A part can mix several things. Official summaries sometimes describe a change, the reason for it, and how things work today in one paragraph. If a part mixes things you'd answer differently, choose Pass and add your own statement. Answers on a mixed part aren't a precise view of each piece.

Today: these pages and their official content are live. Your votes and statements are practice: they stay on your device and aren't counted. Counting starts once real voter checks exist and a host runs it.

Issue pages, Following, and your own records

Each item on Issues has one permanent issue page. At the top it says what the item is, who can act on it, where it stands in the official record, and the most useful thing you can do next. Every part of the page is labeled Live, Practice, Sample, or Planned.

  • What this is for, today. Issue pages and Following help you take part and keep track, on your own. They can't yet show what constituents want as a group, or whether an office followed its constituents' wishes. That needs counted participation from verified voters and offices answering in public, which are planned.
  • Official developments. The steps listed under "What happened next" come from the official record: Congress.gov and Open States action histories, Federal Register comment dates, and local agendas. They aren't responses to anyone's request, and Civic Accord never says a request caused an action.
  • Responses to requests. None are recorded yet, because offices haven't joined. When they can, a response will be kept apart from what the office commits to do and what the record shows it did. No response never means opposition.
  • Comparing a request with what an office did (planned). Civic Accord doesn't compare live requests with official actions yet, and code never assigns these labels. A future comparison will need a specific request with its version; a specific official action with its source; a plain explanation of which parts match and which don't; a clear line between what the office says about itself and a comparison reviewed by independent reviewers; a way to correct mistakes; and the label "Not yet comparable" when the evidence isn't enough. The sample issue shows one such label, "Partially matches the request," for a fictional office.
  • Following. When you follow an issue, your browser saves what you saw and when. Each time you open Following or the issue page, it compares that with the latest official data and lists what changed and why. Nothing is checked in the background. If the source doesn't answer, the page says "Couldn't check right now"; if the source has no newer data, it says so. Neither is treated as "no change."
  • "I sent it" records. After you write to an office or comment officially, you can keep a record of exactly what you sent, to which office, and when. Offices are recorded by their seat (for example, a state house district), with the officeholder at the time. Every record is marked "reported by you": Civic Accord didn't deliver it and can't confirm it was received.
  • Where it's kept. Only in this browser. Your follows and records aren't sent to Civic Accord; to check for changes, your browser asks Civic Accord's data service for each followed issue's latest public record, the same as opening its page. Records don't sync across your devices and are lost if you clear this browser's data. On the Following page you can export a copy, import it again, or delete all follows and records (your districts, name, and reactions stay; "Forget me on this device" clears those too). Exported files may contain private messages and your views, so keep them safe.
  • Sharing. A shared issue link contains only the issue's public ID, never your views, messages, or address. Shared text includes the source, the date, and that no votes are counted yet.

Today: issue pages, official developments, Following, and your own records are live. A sample issue page, with a fictional office and invented numbers, shows counted results, delivery, and an office's response, all marked Sample.

Contacting your representatives

On any item, you can choose Support, Oppose, or Unsure, add your own words, and Civic Accord writes a short message to the right offices. State legislators' published emails come from Open States. Email opens in your own email app. Members of Congress don't publish email addresses, so one button copies the message as it opens their official contact form, and you paste it there. Your name and town are remembered on your device as you type them, so later messages show "Signed as" with a Change link. For proposed rules, the official comment to the agency is what counts, so that comes first. The sample "Ask your representatives" page asks offices to adopt a tool like this one; it asks for your name each time and uses a separate copy button.

About the "Ask" page. It asks each office to change how it hears from residents. It doesn't ask anyone to support a particular bill or spending, and it says so in the message. It asks you to add a sentence in your own words, because offices often give more weight to messages in a person's own words, and the message says it started from a suggestion on Civic Accord. Until a host runs Civic Accord, this page is a sample that isn't being promoted. Before a host promotes it widely, the host's lawyers will check it against the rules for nonprofits, which limit how much they may try to influence legislation.

Today: reactions stay on your device. In the live version, verified voters' reactions would be counted in the public record.

Finding your governments (My representatives)

The My representatives page asks for your address only after explaining why. Your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau; it never reaches Civic Accord. Only your districts and the names of your governments are kept, in your browser, so you don't have to enter the address again and Issues knows which items are yours. It lists members of Congress and your state legislators with the contact details they publish, and says clearly where local officeholders aren't covered yet. Opened from an issue page, it offers a link back to that issue.

In 2025 Google shut down a free service that many apps used to find elected officials. Civic Accord's open replacement works like this:

  1. The U.S. Census Bureau's free address lookup turns your address into districts: congressional, state house and senate, county, city or town, and school district.
  2. An open registry describes each office: its branch, how it's chosen, and whether it can receive proposals. It covers all 50 states and D.C., including special cases like Nebraska's one-chamber legislature, D.C. and territories, independent cities, and states without county governments.
  3. Towns and townships are shown when the Census Bureau lists them as working governments. That includes New England towns, New Jersey and Pennsylvania townships, and places where an address is inside both a town or township and a village or city, as in New York, Michigan, and Ohio. Both are shown then, since each has its own elected officials. What each one handles depends on state law. Where a town only repeats a city or borough, it's shown once.
  4. Names of members of Congress come from the public-domain congress-legislators data. State legislators come from Open States. Party is never shown.

Special districts (water, fire, transit) aren't in the Census data yet. Local hosts can add them.

Rules come from law

Every rule the tool applies comes from that government's own law or chamber rules, with the source shown. Examples are the limits on official mass communications before elections, and what voter-list information may be used. Where no law applies, the tool adds no rule of its own. Rules not yet confirmed are labeled. Legal review happens in two layers: law that's the same everywhere is reviewed once by the host's lawyer, and each state's rules are reviewed by local experts, such as a law school clinic, before launch and each election cycle.

Security

No single test proves a system secure. Civic Accord is checked in layers, and the most important is a design where the public can check results even if part of the system is attacked.

  • Checkable by design (planned): a public record anyone can recount, private receipts that show your vote is included without showing how you voted, identity kept apart from votes, and AI that never counts.
  • Open code that anyone can inspect (works now).
  • Automatic checks on every change (a first set works now: the code is valid, pages run no code mixed into them, and the address lookup's security rules match; more are planned).
  • Independent review before launch: a security review and break-in test against the OWASP ASVS standard, by experts with no stake in the project. Code written with AI help gets the same scrutiny.
  • Private problem reporting through GitHub (security policy), and later a bug bounty.
  • Recurring audits with public reports (planned).

Today: the site sends strict security headers, and its pages run only their own script files, never code mixed into the page. The Census Bureau's address lookup runs in a separate, sealed-off frame that can't reach the rest of the site, your saved settings, or anything you type elsewhere. Five data functions run on the server. Four answer visitors and only read public data. The fifth runs on a schedule, only the hosting service's scheduler can start it, and it saves public state bill lists, shared call counts, and nothing personal to a private store. Their keys are stored as secrets. No real votes or identities are collected.

Using it as an app

First visit. The first time a browser opens Civic Accord, the home page shows a short welcome card (three things to know) and the first issue page shows a full note on where its information comes from. After that, the card stays hidden and the note shrinks to a line. See the welcome card again. "Forget me on this device" also brings it back.

Civic Accord can be added to a phone's home screen and opens full screen, like an app. It's the same website, so it's always current, and there's no app store in between. Pages you've opened work without a signal. Live information, like Issues and the lookup, always comes fresh from its source and is never shown from an old saved copy. App-store versions may come later, once there's a host to own those accounts.

Every page has the same four main places: Home, Issues, My representatives, and Following. On a phone they sit in a menu at the bottom of the screen. How it works and Learn are at the top of each page and in its footer.

Who runs it

Civic Accord was started by a resident acting as a convener, not an owner. The goal is for a neutral university or nonpartisan nonprofit to host and run it, with the convener in an advisory role. Before real use, each deliberation pack will be checked by two reviewers, published, and open to public objection for 7 days before it opens (how reviews work). Every change to the code and data is already kept in the public history.

The charter. A short public charter says who holds the keys and what can't change without a process (read it). Today the convener holds the domain and accounts in trust. Planned: three to five stewards from different organizations, and any change to the accounts, the core design, or the host needs two of them to agree. When a host takes over, everything transfers to it at no cost. There is no company, membership, token, or treasury behind the project, and nothing is for sale.

The decision log. Every governance decision is written down with its date, reason, and who agreed, and entries are never removed (read the log).

Conflicts of interest. Anyone deciding something for the project discloses any stake and steps aside from that decision. The convener has disclosed stakes in short-term rentals and farming and takes no part in reviewing packs on housing or agriculture.

Anyone can read, use, and change the code. If someone runs a changed copy as a public website, the license requires them to share their changed code too, so anyone can check what it does (GNU AGPL 3.0). Anyone can reuse the registry and pack data if they credit Civic Accord (CC BY 4.0). The licenses don't cover the name: a copy shouldn't present itself as the official Civic Accord.
