What it is

Civic Accord is a free, nonpartisan tool for a two-way relationship between residents and their governments. It shows what your governments have open for public input. It lets verified residents talk an issue through, finds where people with different views agree, shows what each choice would cost, and sends the result to the office that represents them. The office is asked to answer in public.

It works for any U.S. address and every level of government: federal, state, county, city or town, and school district. It is advisory. It doesn't replace voting or elections, and it doesn't make laws.

The six steps

  1. Learn. Everyone reads the same short facts. Every fact links to its source.
  2. Weigh in. Verified residents agree, disagree, or pass on short statements, and can add their own. There's no reply button, so no one can pile on. Votes count only after you press Submit, and you get a receipt (sample, in this example).
  3. Common ground. The tool shows what people with different views agree on, and where they split.
  4. Weigh costs. Each option shows its total cost, its cost per resident, and how the estimate was made. You can turn options on and off and see the total change.
  5. Draft. A starting bill is drafted from the common ground. In this example, simple code assembles it; in the live version, AI would help write it. Ideas that divide people are flagged, not hidden.
  6. Hand off. The package goes to the office. You can follow what happens next on a public timeline.

Who can take part

Registered voters decide. Registered voters who live in the district make up the official result: the one that shapes the draft and goes to the office. That matches the people who elect the office.

Other residents can still take part. Residents 16 and up who aren't registered, including 16- and 17-year-olds, can weigh in. Their views travel with the proposal, shown separately and clearly labeled. They aren't part of the official result. Sixteen is the age many states let people preregister to vote.

Proving you live there. You enter your address, then confirm it by a code mailed to that address, or with a quick check at a public library. In this example, the code is shown on screen instead of mailed.

Voter registration status. Your status (registered, preregistered, or not registered) is checked using only what your state's law allows. In Hawaiʻi, for example, a voter's name, district, and status are public, so only those are matched. The strongest method is an official yes-or-no answer from the state election office. Where a state's rules haven't been confirmed yet, status is self-reported and labeled that way. In this example, no real match is made.

Two views, one official. Results show the official result from registered voters, with other residents' views one click away. Offices receive both, labeled.

Your privacy

  • Your address never reaches Civic Accord's servers. Your browser sends it straight to the U.S. Census Bureau's free lookup to find your districts. Only your districts are kept.
  • Your identity is kept apart from your votes. In the live version, the residency check gives your device a sealed voting key. The vote record sees only that key, never your name. It's designed so even the host can't link the two.
  • Messages to your representatives come from you. Email opens in your own email app. Civic Accord never sees or keeps what you send.
  • Looking up your legislators sends only district numbers, never an address.
  • Today, everything you do stays on your own device (your districts, reactions, and sample verification). Nothing is sent back to Civic Accord.
  • No ads or analytics. The site doesn't track you. Its fonts load from Google Fonts, and sharing sites like Facebook have their own policies if you use their share buttons.

How votes are counted

In the live version, votes go into a public record that can be added to but never quietly changed. Simple code that anyone can read does the counting, so anyone can download the record and recount it. Your private receipt lets you find your own votes in it without revealing who you are. The plan is to use established, open methods for this, such as Microsoft's open-source ElectionGuard, rather than inventing new ones.

Each voter record can be claimed by only one verified person. Before a package goes to an office, voter status is checked again against the current list.

Today: the example uses sample votes, and receipts are generated on your device to show how they would work.

How common ground is found

Participants are sorted into groups by how similarly they vote. The live version will use the open method from Pol.is, which Taiwan's vTaiwan process uses. In the example, the groups come with the sample data. Each statement is then labeled with these exact rules:

LabelRule
Common groundAt least 70% of both groups agree.
Divides groupsAt least 70% of one group agrees, and under 50% of the other does.
Still formingAnything else, or fewer than 10 votes so far.

Citizen panels. A randomly chosen panel, balanced to match the district by age and area, votes too. Its result is shown beside the open results, using these rules: the panel agrees at 70% or more, is divided at 40 to 69%, and disagrees under 40%. When the panel and the open group differ, the tool says so. A table shows who took part compared with the district.

How costs are shown

Every option shows how much, times the cost of each unit, equals the total. For example: 12 inspectors × $85,000 = $1.02 million a year. It also shows the cost per resident, using the official population figure. Every estimate says how it was made and links to its source. Yearly and one-time costs are kept separate. Fees and fines that could pay for it are subtracted only when there's a reliable estimate. Anything that can't be estimated is shown as "not yet estimated," never guessed.

What AI does and doesn't do

AI mayAI never
Draft a starting bill from the common groundCounts votes, or touches the vote record
Help organize and summarizeChecks residency or voter status
Help write issue packs, which a person then reviewsWrites or frames the items on Open Now, which come straight from official sources
Decides anything. People decide, and the office decides whether to act.

Every AI summary links to the votes behind it, so you don't have to take it on trust.

The office, not the person

The conversation is with an office (a seat), not an individual politician. Responses are signed by the office and name who approved them. When someone new is elected, every open package, request, and past response passes to them. Offices answer publicly with a reason. They can ask for changes, which residents then weigh in on, and they report each step: introduced, heard, voted. Offices can also ask their district to deliberate, through a neutral host. There's no party branding, no campaign use, and no ratings of officials.

Open Now: where items come from

Open Now lists what your governments have open for public input. It comes straight from these sources, with no AI involved, and refreshes every few hours:

WhatSource
Proposed federal rules open for public commentFederal Register (no key needed)
Recently active bills in CongressCongress.gov
Bills still moving in your state legislatureOpen States. Shown only while the legislature is in session. Bills already signed, vetoed, or finished are left out.
City, county, and school board agendasComing as local hosts connect their governments' public feeds

Each item shows its source and when it was fetched, and links to the official text and the government's own comment channel.

Contacting your representatives

On any item, you can choose Support, Oppose, or Unsure, add your own words, and Civic Accord writes a short message to the right offices. State legislators' published emails come from Open States. Email opens in your own email app. Members of Congress don't publish email addresses, so the tool copies the message and opens their official contact form. For proposed rules, the official comment to the agency is what counts, so that comes first. The "Ask your representatives" page works the same way, asking offices to adopt a tool like this one.

Today: reactions stay on your device. In the live version, verified residents' reactions would be counted in the public record.

Finding your governments

Google shut down its free Representatives API in 2025. Civic Accord's open replacement works like this:

  1. The U.S. Census Bureau's free geocoder turns your address into districts: congressional, state house and senate, county, city or town, and school district.
  2. An open registry describes each office: its branch, how it's chosen, and whether it can receive proposals. It covers all 50 states and D.C., including special cases like Nebraska's one-chamber legislature, D.C. and territories, independent cities, and states without county governments.
  3. Names of members of Congress come from the public-domain congress-legislators data. State legislators come from Open States. Party is never shown.

Special districts (water, fire, transit) aren't in the Census data yet. Local hosts can add them.

Rules come from law

Every rule the tool applies comes from that government's own law or chamber rules, with the source shown. Examples are the limits on official mass communications before elections, and what voter-list information may be used. Where no law applies, the tool adds no rule of its own. Rules not yet confirmed are labeled. Legal review happens in two layers: law that's the same everywhere is reviewed once by the host's lawyer, and each state's rules are reviewed by local experts, such as a law school clinic, before launch and each election cycle.

Security

No single test proves a system secure. Civic Accord is checked in layers, and the most important is a design where the public can check results even if part of the system is attacked.

  • Checkable by design: a public record anyone can recount, private receipts, identity kept apart from votes, and AI that never counts.
  • Open code that anyone can inspect.
  • Automatic checks on every change.
  • Independent review before launch: a security review and break-in test against the OWASP ASVS standard, by experts with no stake in the project. Code written with AI help gets the same scrutiny.
  • Private problem reporting through GitHub (security policy), and later a bug bounty.
  • Recurring audits with public reports.

Today: the site sends strict security headers, its two data functions only read public data, and their keys are stored as secrets. No real votes or identities are collected.

Who runs it

Civic Accord was started by a resident acting as a convener, not an owner. The goal is for a neutral university or nonpartisan nonprofit to host and run it, with the convener in an advisory role. Before real use, each deliberation pack will be reviewed by a second person before it opens. Every change to the code and data is already kept in the public history.
