Civic Accord Learn

For anyone who can't vote yet, or is just learning how government works: young people, new residents, and anyone not registered. Learn with real issues your governments are deciding right now, and take real action that's open to everyone.

Separate from voting Nothing you do here is counted in Civic Accord's deliberations, which only verified voters take part in. You need no account. Your notes and topic picks stay on this device. If you enter an address, it goes straight from your browser to the U.S. Census Bureau to find your districts, and only the districts are kept here.

Your voice counts before you can vote

Here's what anyone can do, whatever their age or whether they can vote:

  1. Comment on proposed federal rules. Federal agencies must take public comments, and "absolutely anyone" can submit one, according to Regulations.gov.
  2. Write to the people who represent you. Officials represent everyone who lives in their district, not just voters.
  3. Testify on bills. Many state legislatures accept testimony from the public when a bill has a hearing. In Hawaiʻi, for example, anyone with a free account can submit testimony online. Testimony is public.
  4. Go to public meetings. City councils, county councils, and school boards hold meetings that anyone can attend, and most set aside time for public comment.
  5. Get ready to vote. Many states let you preregister before you turn 18. Enter an address below to see the age where you live.

If you're under 13, ask a parent, guardian, or teacher before you write to an office or submit a comment online.

How decisions get made

A bill becomes a law

  1. A legislator introduces a bill.
  2. A committee holds a hearing. This is often when the public can testify.
  3. The committee votes. Many bills stop here.
  4. The whole chamber votes.
  5. The other chamber does the same. Nebraska has just one chamber.
  6. The governor, or for Congress the president, signs or vetoes it.

A rule gets made

  1. An agency proposes a rule to carry out a law.
  2. It's published, and a comment period opens.
  3. Anyone can comment.
  4. The agency must consider the comments.
  5. It publishes a final rule, which may be changed because of the comments.

A local decision

  1. The council or board posts an agenda before the meeting.
  2. The public can usually comment, in person or in writing.
  3. Members discuss and vote in public.
  4. Minutes and votes are posted afterward.

Practice finding common ground

Try a sample deliberation in practice mode. You can vote on statements and see how people with different views can still agree. Practice votes use sample data, stay on your device, and are never counted.

Start a practice deliberation

For teachers and groups

  • Pick a real item from the list above and read it together using the 5 questions.
  • Run the practice deliberation as a class, then talk about where groups agreed and why.
  • Have each student write their own comment or letter. Sending it is each person's choice.
  • Nothing is collected: no accounts, no student data, no tracking. Notes stay in the browser. On shared computers, have students tap Forget me on this device at the bottom of the page when they finish.
