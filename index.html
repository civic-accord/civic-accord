Civic Accord · a citizen-led, open example

A new relationship with your government

Imagine talking an issue through with your neighbors. You see where you actually agree and what each choice would cost. Then you send it to your representatives, who are asked to answer you in public. Civic Accord is a free, open example of how that could work, using tools that already exist.

An example, not a finished product. Everything on this site uses example data. It shows what's possible so residents can ask for it. Before real use, it needs a neutral host, legal review in each state, and independent security audits.

How it works

  1. Learn. Everyone starts from the same short, sourced facts.
  2. Weigh in. Verified residents agree or disagree with short statements, and add their own.
  3. Common ground. The tool shows where people with different views agree, and where they don't.
  4. Weigh costs. Every choice shows its estimated cost and what it would cost each resident.
  5. Draft. AI turns the common ground into a starting bill. People decide what's in it.
  6. Hand off. The proposal goes to the office that represents you. The office is asked to answer publicly, with its reasons.

Trust you can check

This isn't about suspicion or taking sides. Civic Accord doesn't ask you to take anyone's word for it. It shows how residents and their representatives can work on problems together, in a process open enough that anyone can check it.

17%

of Americans trusted the federal government to do what is right most of the time in 2025. Pew Research Center

Openness alone won't change that. But a process people can check for themselves is a place to start.

What today's technology can already do

Each of these already exists. Civic Accord puts them together, and each one comes with a way for you to check it.

Find everyone who represents you

Type an address and see every government above it, from Congress to your school district.

How you can check: the districts come from the U.S. Census Bureau's free, public lookup.

Prove you live there, privately

A code mailed to your address, or a quick check at a library, confirms you're a resident. Your identity is then separated from your votes.

How you can check: your verification receipt proves you were confirmed. It never shows how you voted.

Count votes in the open

Votes go into a public record that can be added to but never quietly changed. Simple code that anyone can read does the counting.

How you can check: anyone can download the record and recount. A separate vote receipt lets only you find your own votes. It never shows your name.

Find common ground, not just a majority

The tool groups people by how they vote and highlights ideas that people on different sides support.

Already used in Taiwan's vTaiwan process, with Pol.is, a free tool whose code anyone can read.

Hear from a fair cross-section

A randomly chosen panel, balanced to match the district, votes alongside everyone who signed up, so results reflect more than the most motivated.

Already used in Ireland's Citizens' Assembly of 99 randomly selected citizens.

Show the cost of every choice

Each option shows its total cost, its cost per resident, and how the estimate was made.

How you can check: every estimate links to its source.

Use AI as a helper, never a judge

AI summarizes and drafts. It never counts votes, and every summary links to the votes behind it.

How you can check: open any summary and see the votes it came from.

Follow what happens next

The office is asked to answer publicly, say what it will do, and report each step: introduced, heard, voted.

How you can check: the response record is public, including "no response yet."

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't replace voting or elections. It's advisory.
  • It doesn't make laws. Your representatives still decide.
  • It doesn't take sides. It's nonpartisan, and has no party branding, campaign use, or ratings of officials.
  • It isn't "impossible to tamper with." Nothing is. It's built so that tampering would be visible and could be checked.

Everything is open

  • The code. Anyone can read every line on GitHub.
  • The data. The list of governments and offices is an open file anyone can review and improve.
  • The rules. Every rule comes from each government's own laws, with the source shown. Each state's rules get their own legal review.
  • Who's behind it. Started by a resident as a convener, not an owner. The goal is for a neutral university or nonprofit to run it.

Ask for it

If you'd like your representatives to offer something like this, tell them. Enter your address, and Civic Accord drafts a short, polite message to every office that represents you. You review it and send it yourself. Offices may reply to you directly.
