Civic Accord, a citizen-led, open example

A new relationship with your government

Imagine talking an issue through with your neighbors. You see where you actually agree and what each choice would cost. Then you send it to your representatives, who are asked to answer you in public. Civic Accord is a free, open example of how that could work, using tools that already exist.

Representatives hear from constituents every day. This makes it easier to be one of them, about what's actually on their desk.

Your government, open now

Governments ask for public input all the time: proposed rules, bills, hearings. Civic Accord gathers what's open from official sources, refreshes it through the day, and puts it in one place for your address.

From each item you can read the official text, comment through the government's own channel, or start a deliberation with your neighbors.

How it works

  1. Learn. Everyone starts from the same short, sourced facts.
  2. Weigh in. Verified voters agree or disagree with short statements, and add their own.
  3. Common ground. The tool shows where people with different views agree, and where they don't.
  4. Weigh costs. Every choice shows its estimated cost and what it would cost each resident.
  5. Draft. A starting bill is drafted from the common ground. People decide what's in it, and the office decides whether to act.
  6. Hand off. The proposal goes to the office that represents you. The office is asked to answer publicly, with its reasons.

Use it like an app

Add Civic Accord to your phone's home screen. It opens full screen, like any app, and it's the same site, always up to date. Nothing to download from an app store.

  • iPhone or iPad: open this page in Safari, tap the Share button, then Add to Home Screen.
  • Android: open this page in Chrome, tap the menu (three dots), then Install app or Add to Home screen.

The front page and How it works open even without a signal. Live items like Open Now always load fresh, so you never see old information as if it were current.

Trust you can check

This isn't about suspicion or taking sides. It's about a working relationship: residents and the offices that represent them, working on real problems together, in a process open enough that anyone can check it.

What it's designed to do

Each of these methods already exists and is used elsewhere. Each card says whether it works in Civic Accord today or is planned, and how you'll be able to check it.

Works now

Find everyone who represents you

Type an address and see every government above it, from Congress to your school district.

How you can check: the districts come from the U.S. Census Bureau's free, public lookup.
Planned

Prove you're a registered voter, privately

Your voter record would be matched, then a code mailed to the address on that record, or a quick check at a library, would confirm you're that voter. Your identity would then be kept apart from your votes, and you'd respond only to your own governments.

How you'll be able to check: your verification receipt will prove you were confirmed, without showing how you voted.
Planned

Count votes in the open

Votes would go into a public record that can be added to but never quietly changed. Simple code that anyone can read would do the counting.

How you'll be able to check: anyone will be able to download the record and recount. A separate vote receipt will let only you find your own votes, without showing your name.
Works with sample votes

Find common ground, not just a majority

The tool groups people by how they vote and highlights ideas that people on different sides support.

Already used in Taiwan's vTaiwan process, with Pol.is, a free tool whose code anyone can read.
Planned

Hear from a fair cross-section

A randomly chosen panel, balanced to match the district, would vote alongside everyone who signed up, so results reflect more than the most motivated.

Already used in Ireland's Citizens' Assembly of 99 randomly selected citizens.
Works with sample issues

Show the cost of every choice

Each option shows its total cost, its cost per resident, and how the estimate was made.

How you can check: every estimate links to its source.
Planned

Use AI as a helper, never a judge

AI would help summarize and draft. It would never count votes, and every summary would link to the votes behind it.

How you'll be able to check: open any summary and see the votes it came from.
Planned

Follow what happens next

The office would be asked to answer publicly, say what it will do, and report each step: introduced, heard, voted.

How you'll be able to check: the response record will be public, including "no response yet." You can try it in the sample office inbox.

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't replace voting or elections. It's advisory.
  • It doesn't make laws. Your representatives still decide.
  • It doesn't take sides. It's nonpartisan, and has no party branding, campaign use, or ratings of officials.
  • It isn't "impossible to tamper with." Nothing is. It's built so that tampering would be visible and could be checked.

Everything is open

  • The code. Anyone can read every line on GitHub.
  • The data. The list of governments and offices is an open file anyone can review and improve.
  • The rules. Every rule comes from each government's own laws, with the source shown. Each state's rules get their own legal review.
  • How it works. Every detail, in plain language, on the How it works page.
  • Who's behind it. Started by a resident as a convener, not an owner. The goal is for a neutral university or nonprofit to run it.

Ask for it

If you'd like your representatives to offer something like this, tell them. Enter your address, and Civic Accord drafts a short, polite message to every office that represents you. You review it and send it yourself. Offices may reply to you directly.
